## The Hidden Weakness of Hard Stones

When beginners look at the Mohs Scale of Hardness, they often confuse "hardness" with "toughness." A diamond, ranking as a 10 on the Mohs scale, is the hardest natural material on Earth—meaning absolutely nothing can scratch it except another diamond. 

However, if you take a diamond and hit it with a steel hammer, it will shatter into a dozen pieces. Steel is only a 5.5 on the Mohs scale, so how can it destroy a 10?

The answer lies in a critical physical property of crystalline minerals known as **cleavage**. Understanding cleavage is the difference between successfully faceting a valuable gemstone and watching it split in half on the lapidary machine.

---

## 1. What is a Cleavage Plane?

To understand cleavage, you must imagine the atomic structure of a gemstone. In a crystal lattice, atoms are arranged in highly organized, repeating geometric patterns, held together by atomic bonds.

In many minerals, the strength of these bonds is not equal in all directions. 

A **cleavage plane** is a flat, two-dimensional plane running through the crystal where the atomic bonds are significantly weaker or spaced further apart than in other directions. If physical stress (like a sharp blow or excessive grinding pressure) is applied parallel to this weak plane, the crystal will cleanly split apart, leaving a perfectly flat, mirror-like surface.

Think of chopping firewood: hitting the log parallel to the grain easily splits the wood in half. Hitting the log perpendicular to the grain just causes the axe to bounce or get stuck. Cleavage planes are the "grain" of a gemstone.

---

## 2. Types and Ratings of Cleavage

Gemologists rate cleavage based on how easily the stone splits and how smooth the resulting surface is.

* **Perfect / Eminent Cleavage:** The stone splits effortlessly, leaving a perfectly flat, highly reflective surface. (Examples: Topaz, Diamond, Fluorite).
* **Good / Distinct Cleavage:** The stone splits relatively easily, but the resulting surface may be slightly stepped or uneven. (Example: Feldspar).
* **Poor / Indistinct Cleavage:** The stone is difficult to split along the plane, and the break is highly irregular. (Example: Beryl/Aquamarine).
* **None:** The stone has no planes of weakness. When it breaks, it fractures randomly like glass (conchoidal fracture). (Examples: Quartz, Opal, Corundum/Sapphire).

---

## 3. The Diamond Cutter's Secret

Historically, the perfect cleavage of diamonds was the only way early jewelers could shape the stone. Because they didn't have motorized diamond saws, a master diamond cutter (a *cleaver*) would examine the rough stone to determine the exact angle of the octahedral cleavage planes.

The cleaver would scratch a tiny groove into the stone, place a dull steel blade into the groove, and strike it firmly with a mallet. If the angle was perfectly aligned with the cleavage plane, the diamond would instantly and cleanly split into two usable pieces. If the angle was wrong, the diamond would shatter into worthless dust.

Today, while lasers do most of the cutting, facetors must still be intensely aware of the cleavage planes. You cannot polish a facet that lies perfectly parallel to a cleavage plane; the microscopic layers will simply flake off rather than polish, a frustrating phenomenon known as "polishing against the grain."

---

## 4. Topaz: The Lapidary's Headache

For the everyday facetor, **Topaz** is the ultimate lesson in managing perfect cleavage. 

Topaz has a Mohs hardness of 8, making it highly resistant to scratching and excellent for daily-wear jewelry. However, it possesses one direction of *perfect basal cleavage*, meaning it splits perfectly flat across the base of its crystal structure.

### How to Cut Around the Cleavage
When a facetor receives a piece of rough topaz, they cannot simply cut it in any direction. If the large, flat top facet (the table) of the finished gem is cut exactly parallel to the cleavage plane, the stone will be incredibly fragile. A simple bump against a table could cause the entire top of the gem to shear off.

To prevent this, the cutter deliberately tilts the design. They ensure that the table facet is cut at a 5-degree to 15-degree angle *away* from the cleavage plane. This slight misalignment significantly increases the toughness of the finished gem, ensuring it can survive being set into a ring and worn safely.

### Setting Stones with Cleavage
Jewelers must also respect cleavage. When setting a stone like Topaz, Kunzite, or Tanzanite, the jeweler must use gentle, even pressure when pressing the metal prongs over the stone. Uneven pressure applied directly onto a cleavage plane will instantly crack the stone in the setting.

Cleavage planes are nature's invisible fault lines. For the lapidary artist, learning to read the rough and respect these atomic weaknesses is the key to mastering the craft and protecting your most valuable investments.
